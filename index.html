01

Sept to Dec 2024 · Course Project · Michigan Tech Cleanroom

Integrated NMOS Transistor Fabrication

My first full process flow: bare wafers in, diced NMOS dies out, and a clear list of reasons the devices did not all match.

The Flow

Working in an ISO-classified cleanroom, I took wafers through two-step oxidation, photolithography, a WSi2 gate patterned with RIE, source and drain diffusion, and Cr/Au metallization (50 nm Cr, 200 nm Au), then diced them into 2 mm x 6 mm dies.

To see how the junctions responded to thermal budget, I compared two diffusion splits: 950 °C for 30 minutes and 780 °C for 4 hours.

What the Measurements Showed

  • 29.2 nmGate oxide by ellipsometry
  • 36.3 nmSame oxide by Filmetrics
  • 30 nmTarget

Two tools disagreeing by 7 nm on the same film meant at least one of them was out of calibration, which made every later thickness number harder to trust. Even so, the electrical data pointed the same way: threshold voltage variation tracked gate oxide thickness.

Lithography had its own drift. The UV lamp intensity moved between 9 and 15 mW/cm² around a target dose of about 79 mJ/cm², so exposure was not as repeatable as it looked on paper. At the junctions, incomplete RIE oxide removal before diffusion and WSi2 slag after the 950 °C, 30 minute diffusion likely affected consistency.

Being upfront: the passivation step was not done, and I do not have quantified yield figures for this run.

What I'd Do Differently

  • Calibrate the measurement tools before trusting the numbers they give.
  • Document device geometry during the run. I had to estimate source and drain areas afterward.
  • Hold exposure dose, RIE clearing, and diffusion conditions to tighter limits.

Tools and Techniques: Thermal Oxidation, Diffusion, UV Photolithography, RIE, Cr/Au Metallization, Ellipsometry, Filmetrics, Dicing, Electrical Testing

02

Jan to Apr 2025 · Course Project · Team of Four

Test Chip Fabrication, Packaging, and Interposer Design

A passive test chip taken from bare oxide to a packaged die, plus the PCB interposer meant to carry its signals out to test hardware.

Part One: On the Wafer

We sputtered 30 nm of Cr and 300 nm of Au at 100 W RF onto 330 nm of SiO2, then patterned about 1.5 µm of SU-8. Rather than guess at the lithography settings, I ran a small DOE across exposure time (12 to 14 s) and development time (35 to 50 s) and compared the results under the microscope.

After plasma etching and dicing, we attached dies to QFN72 packages with a 150 °C, 15 minute cure. Throughout, I tuned tool setup and parameters under faculty supervision. I also prepared the wire bonder and updated its SOP, although wedge bonding was not completed within lab time.

  • 2Serpentine resistors within 1% of their 100 Ω and 120 Ω targets
  • 0Usable capacitor measurements

I measured the resistor structures on a Keithley parameter analyzer. The other resistors deviated more from their targets, and the capacitors gave no usable results.

Part Two: The Interposer

To connect the dies to outside test hardware, our team designed a PCB interposer in KLayout and Altium Designer, working within Sunstone Circuits' manufacturing limits. It was a 2-layer, 2 in x 2 in board with 1 oz copper, 6/6 mil trace and space, 30/15 mil vias, and two 500 mil x 500 mil BGA footprints. An isolated ground plane sat under 10 mil signal traces routed with balanced lengths.

The boards came back, but assembly did not work. Ground pads had been misread as solder-mask-free areas, some pad and trace sizes were out of tolerance, and die attach failed: the pads were too small to place a die by hand, and they had no self-aligning shape to let solder pull the die into position.

What I'd Do Differently

  • Run a small DOE on resist exposure and development before committing a full wafer.
  • Check every layout against the vendor's design rules before ordering, not after.
  • Use self-aligning pad designs whenever parts will be placed by hand.
  • Keep SOPs current. A shared tool is much faster to set up when the last person's notes are accurate.

Tools and Techniques: RF PVD Sputtering, SU-8 Photolithography, DOE, Plasma Etching, Wafer Dicing, Die Attach, Wire Bonder, Keithley Parameter Analyzer, KLayout, Altium Designer

03

Sept to Dec 2025 · Directed Study

FE-SEM and EIS Pinhole Screening

An attempt at a faster, non-destructive way to find nanoscale pinholes in sub-35 nm SiO2 films, by pairing FE-SEM imaging with Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy.

The Approach

Imaging alone covers a small area at a time, so I wanted an electrical screen that could flag a film before it went under the FE-SEM. After reviewing pinhole characterization methods, I chose EIS for that role.

I prepared a bare silicon reference and SiO2 films at two deposition times. Ellipsometry fits put them at about 11 nm and 33 nm. I diced the wafers into coupons and set up a three-electrode cell on a potentiostat to run impedance sweeps.

Where It Ended

The directed study ended at graduation without final, validated results.

The early sweeps did not line up with film thickness: the thicker film looked electrically close to bare silicon. I had not yet confirmed the cause with FE-SEM imaging or repeat runs, so I treat those readings as open questions rather than conclusions.

What I Took From It

  • A thickness measurement alone does not tell you whether a dielectric film is intact.
  • EIS gives an area-averaged signal, so it needs imaging alongside it to locate and confirm defects.

Tools and Techniques: EIS, ThermoFisher Apreo2 FE-SEM, Ellipsometry, Dicing
